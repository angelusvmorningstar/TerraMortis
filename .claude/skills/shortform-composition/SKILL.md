# Short-form Composition

House style for compact in-world **narrative**. The goal of any scene is **a moment of
meaning for the character**, written in the fewest words that carry it.

## Scope — read this first

This skill is for **scenes with drama**: Story Moments, touchstone vignettes, POV drafts
of a shared event, rumours. Craft is wanted here.

It is **NOT** for the **result of an action** — what a contact reported, what an
investigate turned up, what a merit action achieved. Those are **reports**, and they have
their own skill: **`procedural-report`**. Applying scene craft to a result glosses names,
dramatises findings, and invents facts to complete a cadence. That has happened and it put
wrong information in front of a player.

**Angelus says "narrative" → this skill. Angelus says "procedural" → `procedural-report`.**
Scenes get craft. Results get reported.

## Non-negotiable rules

1. **Parsimony.** Use the fewest words that land the moment. More words means more
   surface to get wrong. When in doubt, cut. After a draft, do a deletion pass and
   remove anything the meaning survives without.
2. **Direct language. No metaphor, no ornamentation.** State things plainly. Do not
   bury meaning in indirect or "poetic" phrasing. If a sentence can be read two ways,
   rewrite it so it can be read one way.
3. **Never invent plot to manufacture a story.** Do not add events, conflicts, NPCs,
   turns, or PC-on-PC manipulation the player did not submit. The drama comes from
   what is already true about the character, not from a beat bolted on.
4. **Meaning, not texture.** A scene must land something real for the character: a
   recognition, a choice, a feeling that costs them. Atmosphere alone is not a scene.
   Draw the meaning from the character's own established history and what they wrote.
5. **Identity is concrete; feeling is implied.** This is the omission rule. Productive
   omission is a gap the reader can close — leave the *emotion* unsaid and trust them
   to feel it. A referent the reader cannot place (a name, "the young ones", a private
   object) is not omission, it is a missing antecedent that stalls the read. So:
   **every person or object gets a self-contained tag at first mention, or it is cut.**
   No name without a handle (who they are to this character, in one clause).
   **Check the opening line specifically, every time, before showing a draft:** a scene
   must never open on a bare pronoun ("He finds you...", "It arrives...") with no noun
   yet introduced to attach it to. Introduce the concrete noun first ("A monk finds
   you...", "An email arrives...") and let pronouns follow. This has recurred twice in
   one session (Hazel's "The Monk," Keeper's "Marks and Signs") — it is cheap to catch
   and easy to miss without a deliberate check, so make the check deliberate.
6. **Strong, exact nouns and verbs.** Specificity creates immersion in few words.
   Prefer the precise concrete noun over an adjective stack.
7. **One moment, one effect.** Single focus. Do not seed multiple unexplained threads
   in a short piece. Start in the meaningful moment, not in preamble.

## Project conventions (always)

- British English (Defence, Honour, Socialise, capitalise, colour).
- No em-dashes.
- No "G'day", no cringe slang.
- Refer to characters by moniker where one exists.

## Workflow loop (one scene at a time)

1. **Propose the scenario as bullets.** Calibrate to the actual characters and the
   staging (where, when, who is present, what is already true going in). Name the
   **moment of meaning** the scene will land — not a plot twist. Keep proposals
   short.
2. **Wait for sign-off.** Do not draft prose until the user approves or redirects the
   moment. If the proposed meaning is invented or thin, say so and back up.
3. **Draft one POV at a time.** When signed off, write a draft per point-of-view
   character. The underlying substance can overlap (it is one shared event), but each
   POV is distinct in what that character notices, knows, and feels. Apply every rule
   above to each draft.
4. **Deletion pass before delivery.** Re-read each draft and cut. Confirm every
   referent is tagged and the meaning is implied, not explained.

## Source basis

The omission rule (productive omission vs missing antecedent; identity concrete,
feeling implied) is drawn from flash/micro-fiction craft: single effect, the reader
completes the story, strong exact nouns, every remaining element carries definable
weight. References:
- Baltimore Review — Concision and Precision in Flash Fiction
- Pif Magazine — The Essentials of Micro-Fiction
- NYC Midnight — How to Write Microfiction
